# Clinical Ontology: Project Layers Operational System Mapping

## Ontology purpose

The Project Layers ontology is an operational mapping guide for converting structured, synthetic case facts into provisional systems-level hypotheses. It is not a diagnostic taxonomy. A domain activation means that the domain may be useful to consider; it does not confirm a cause, condition, disorder, or diagnosis.

## Mapping principles

- Use tentative wording such as “may suggest,” “could indicate,” “might be relevant,” “consider clarifying,” and “appears consistent with.”
- Do not map any single reported indicator directly to a diagnosis.
- Treat all activations as hypotheses requiring clinician review.
- Show the entered facts and rule IDs behind every activation.
- Consider competing explanations and contextual factors.
- Model strengths and environmental factors as active formulation elements, not afterthoughts.
- Include medical/referral considerations without producing medical conclusions.

## Operational domain template

Each Project Layers domain is described using:

- Domain name.
- Plain-language definition.
- Possible reported indicators.
- Information that would strengthen the hypothesis.
- Information that would weaken the hypothesis.
- Common maintaining processes.
- Appropriate clarification questions.
- Possible therapeutic targets.
- Relevant medical considerations.
- Cautions against overinterpretation.

## 1. Medical and physiological contributors

### Plain-language definition

Bodily, health, medication, sleep, pain, neurological, endocrine/metabolic, reproductive/hormonal, substance-related, or other physiological factors that may be relevant to the client’s current experience and may warrant clarification, assessment, consultation, or referral as clinically indicated.

### Possible reported indicators

- Recent changes in sleep, appetite, energy, pain, dizziness, headaches, gastrointestinal symptoms, sensory changes, or physical functioning.
- Medication start, stop, dose change, adherence change, or side-effect concern.
- Substance intoxication, reduction, withdrawal concern, or change in use pattern.
- Recent illness, injury, surgery, concussion, pregnancy/postpartum context, or unexplained physical symptoms.
- New confusion, sudden cognitive change, acute neurological change, or sudden personality/behavior change.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Clear timing between physiological change and reported distress or functioning change.
- Medical history, recent labs, medication changes, or clinician observations suggesting possible physiological contribution.
- Symptoms fluctuating with sleep loss, pain, substances, medication timing, illness, hormonal changes, or nutrition/hydration.
- Collateral or medical documentation supporting a need for assessment.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Stable health, sleep, medication, and substance context with no meaningful temporal relationship to the presenting concern.
- Physical symptoms already assessed and monitored by appropriate medical professionals, while the current formulation is better explained by other domains.
- Reported concerns occur consistently across physiological states without notable variation.

### Common maintaining processes

- Sleep disruption, pain, substances, medication effects, or health anxiety potentially increasing arousal and reducing coping capacity.
- Physical symptoms leading to avoidance, reduced activity, or increased threat monitoring.
- Uncertainty about bodily symptoms increasing reassurance seeking or rumination.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What changed physically, medically, or medication-wise around the time this became more difficult?”
- “How have sleep, pain, appetite, substances, and energy been recently?”
- “Have there been any sudden changes in confusion, speech, movement, sensation, personality, or awareness?”
- “Are there existing medical providers involved, and is consultation indicated?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Coordination with medical care as clinically indicated.
- Psychoeducation about mind-body interactions without asserting causality.
- Sleep, recovery, pacing, and self-monitoring routines within clinical scope.
- Reducing fear-based interpretation of bodily sensations when medically appropriate and clinically reviewed.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Consider further assessment for sudden change in personality or functioning, acute confusion or delirium, neurological symptoms, medication initiation/withdrawal/dosage changes, substance use/intoxication/withdrawal, severe or prolonged sleep loss, endocrine or metabolic concerns, chronic pain, nutritional concerns, unexplained fatigue, head injury, seizure-like events, infection or systemic illness, reproductive or hormonal changes, cognitive decline, physical symptoms temporally linked to emotional changes, or clinician-defined referral triggers.
- Clarify medical history and coordinate with a medical provider as clinically indicated.
- Direct emergencies to established emergency procedures.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Do not conclude that physical factors are the cause of psychological distress.
- Do not state, “This symptom is medically caused.”
- Do not conclude that psychological factors explain physical symptoms.
- Do not provide medical diagnosis, medication advice, or reassurance that medical assessment is unnecessary.

## 2. Nervous-system regulation

### Plain-language definition

Patterns in arousal, activation, shutdown, recovery, sensory sensitivity, and the client’s perceived ability to return toward baseline after stress.

### Possible reported indicators

- Feeling “keyed up,” tense, numb, shut down, flooded, frozen, restless, or easily startled.
- Difficulty calming after conflict, reminders, uncertainty, or sensory stimulation.
- Rapid escalation, prolonged recovery, or alternating high and low activation states.
- Somatic cues such as racing heart, muscle tension, shallow breathing, fatigue, or heaviness.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Repeated reports of arousal shifts across settings.
- Clear triggers and recovery patterns linked to stress, relational cues, sensory load, or perceived threat.
- Clinician observations of activation, shutdown, dissociation-like spacing out, agitation, or fatigue during discussion.
- Improvement with grounding, pacing, breathing, movement, sensory modulation, or co-regulation.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Stable arousal and recovery despite significant stressors.
- Concerns appear primarily driven by external barriers, skill gaps, values conflict, or practical constraints rather than regulation patterns.
- Arousal changes are rare, brief, and not functionally impairing.

### Common maintaining processes

- High arousal narrowing attention and increasing threat interpretation.
- Shutdown reducing problem-solving and connection.
- Avoidance of activation cues reducing opportunities to build tolerance.
- Poor recovery increasing allostatic load over time.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What do you notice in your body when this starts?”
- “How long does it usually take to return toward baseline?”
- “What helps even a little, and what tends to intensify it?”
- “Are there situations where regulation is easier?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Arousal tracking and window-of-tolerance awareness.
- Grounding, paced breathing, sensory modulation, movement, and recovery routines.
- Co-regulation and interpersonal safety practices.
- Gradual capacity building before intensive emotion-focused work.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Consider medical clarification when activation changes are sudden, unexplained, associated with fainting, chest pain, acute neurological symptoms, severe sleep loss, substance changes, or medication changes.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Arousal patterns do not by themselves establish trauma, anxiety, medical illness, or any diagnosis.
- Regulation difficulty may reflect current environment, sleep loss, pain, discrimination, unsafe conditions, or medical factors.

## 3. Allostatic load

### Plain-language definition

The cumulative strain of repeated or chronic demands, especially when recovery time, resources, safety, or support are insufficient.

### Possible reported indicators

- Long-term caregiving burden, work strain, housing instability, financial pressure, legal stress, discrimination, chronic illness, or ongoing relational conflict.
- Feeling depleted, “running on empty,” irritable, overwhelmed, or unable to recover.
- Reduced sleep, fewer restorative activities, and increased reliance on short-term coping.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Multiple concurrent stressors over time with limited recovery.
- Worsening functioning after cumulative demands rather than one isolated event.
- Improvement during respite, support, stability, or reduced demand periods.
- Protective factors are present but insufficient relative to demands.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Adequate recovery, stable resources, and low chronic stress burden.
- Distress is highly circumscribed to a specific cue or belief pattern with minimal cumulative strain.
- Functioning remains stable despite ongoing demands.

### Common maintaining processes

- Depletion reducing emotion regulation, executive functioning, and relational flexibility.
- Chronic stress increasing threat detection and narrowing problem-solving.
- Reduced restorative activity leading to further depletion.
- Environmental demands outpacing available coping resources.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What has been accumulating over the past weeks, months, or years?”
- “Where do you get recovery, and where is recovery blocked?”
- “Which demands feel optional, non-optional, or externally imposed?”
- “What supports reduce the load even temporarily?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Resource mapping and load reduction where possible.
- Recovery planning, pacing, boundaries, and sleep-supportive routines.
- Case-management or environmental intervention.
- Values-based prioritization and support mobilization.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Chronic stress may coexist with sleep, pain, endocrine/metabolic, cardiovascular, immune, or other health concerns; consider consultation or referral when clinically indicated.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Do not frame cumulative stress as individual weakness.
- Avoid implying that coping skills alone can resolve unsafe or structurally constrained conditions.
- Allostatic load is a formulation hypothesis, not a medical measurement unless assessed medically.

## 4. Salience and threat detection

### Plain-language definition

How attention is drawn to possible danger, uncertainty, rejection, loss, failure, or other high-importance cues, and how readily those cues are interpreted as threatening.

### Possible reported indicators

- Scanning for danger, criticism, abandonment, mistakes, bodily sensations, or signs of conflict.
- Difficulty noticing safety cues or neutral information.
- Strong reactions to ambiguous messages, tone, facial expressions, delays, or uncertainty.
- Avoiding situations perceived as unsafe or closely monitoring others’ responses.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Repeated pattern of rapid threat appraisal across contexts.
- Attention remains locked on possible danger despite mixed evidence.
- Client reports difficulty downshifting after ambiguous or minor cues.
- Threat monitoring is linked to avoidance, reassurance seeking, conflict, or exhaustion.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Threat appraisal is proportional to realistic current danger.
- The central issue is practical barrier, grief, values conflict, or skill deficit rather than threat detection.
- Client flexibly updates appraisals when new information appears.

### Common maintaining processes

- Threat scanning increases perceived danger and physiological arousal.
- Ambiguity is interpreted in the most concerning direction.
- Avoidance prevents corrective learning or safety detection.
- Reassurance briefly reduces distress but may increase future checking.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What does your attention go to first in that moment?”
- “What signals tell you things may not be safe?”
- “What evidence points in other possible directions?”
- “Are there times when the same cue feels less threatening?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Attention flexibility and safety-cue detection.
- Threat appraisal review and uncertainty tolerance.
- Grounding before interpretation work.
- Graduated approach to avoided but safe-enough contexts when clinically appropriate.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Sudden changes in vigilance, paranoia-like concerns, confusion, sleep deprivation, substance effects, or medication changes may warrant assessment or consultation as clinically indicated.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Heightened threat detection does not prove trauma exposure or any diagnosis.
- Threat detection may be adaptive in unsafe environments; avoid invalidating realistic danger.

## 5. Interpretive prism and schemas

### Plain-language definition

Recurring templates or assumptions that shape how the client makes sense of events, relationships, self, and future possibilities.

### Possible reported indicators

- Repeated themes such as “I will be rejected,” “I am failing,” “I cannot trust anyone,” “I must be perfect,” or “I am responsible for everything.”
- Similar interpretations across different relationships or settings.
- Strong emotional reactions when a schema-relevant cue appears.
- Difficulty integrating evidence that does not fit the expected pattern.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Consistent cross-situational interpretive pattern.
- Schema-linked predictions influence behavior and relationships.
- Client can identify earlier learning contexts or repeated experiences, while causality remains provisional.
- Alternative interpretations are available but feel less believable.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Interpretations are context-specific, reality-based, and flexible.
- Current environmental conditions sufficiently explain the appraisal without an enduring schema pattern.
- Client readily updates interpretation with new information.

### Common maintaining processes

- Confirmation bias toward schema-consistent evidence.
- Protective behaviors that unintentionally elicit or preserve expected outcomes.
- Avoidance of disconfirming experiences.
- Relational cycles reinforcing familiar expectations.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What did that situation seem to mean about you, others, or what might happen next?”
- “Does this interpretation show up in other parts of life?”
- “What evidence fits, and what evidence does not fit?”
- “What would be a more flexible interpretation that still respects your concerns?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Schema awareness and flexible meaning-making.
- Behavioral experiments or experiential disconfirmation when appropriate.
- Compassionate self-understanding.
- Relational pattern recognition and repair work.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Sudden onset of unusual beliefs, confusion, severe sleep loss, substance effects, or medication changes may warrant assessment or consultation as clinically indicated.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Do not infer personality pathology from schema patterns.
- Schemas may reflect realistic experiences of harm, marginalization, or repeated invalidation.
- A single statement should not be treated as evidence of a stable schema.

## 6. Meaning and cognition

### Plain-language definition

The client’s appraisals, beliefs, narratives, values conflicts, worries, rumination, interpretations of responsibility, and cognitive patterns that may shape distress or action.

### Possible reported indicators

- Persistent worry, rumination, self-criticism, hopeless predictions, responsibility concerns, moral conflict, or difficulty making sense of events.
- Repetitive “what if,” “if only,” or “I should have” thinking.
- Cognitive load interfering with sleep, work, relationships, or decision-making.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Thoughts or meanings reliably intensify emotion, avoidance, or behavior patterns.
- Client reports feeling stuck in repetitive mental loops.
- Distress shifts when meaning, appraisal, or attentional stance shifts.
- Values conflict or loss of meaning is central to the concern.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Distress is primarily linked to immediate external danger, practical needs, physiological changes, or relational events with limited cognitive mediation.
- Client can disengage from thoughts flexibly and remains functionally steady.
- Thoughts are present but not central to distress or behavior.

### Common maintaining processes

- Rumination or worry increases distress without resolving problems.
- Self-critical narratives reduce agency and increase avoidance.
- Values conflict contributes to indecision or disengagement.
- Meaning-making narrows around threat, guilt, shame, or failure.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What does your mind keep returning to?”
- “What does this situation seem to mean?”
- “Which thoughts feel useful, and which feel repetitive or costly?”
- “What values or commitments feel involved?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Cognitive flexibility, defusion, reappraisal, or problem-solving.
- Rumination/worry interruption strategies.
- Values clarification and committed action.
- Self-compassionate meaning reconstruction.

### Relevant medical considerations

- New confusion, memory change, disorganized thinking, severe sleep loss, medication/substance changes, or neurological concerns should prompt consideration of assessment or consultation as clinically indicated.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Difficult thoughts are not diagnoses.
- Cognition-focused targets should not be used to minimize real environmental harm, grief, oppression, or medical concerns.

## 7. Emotion regulation

### Plain-language definition

How the client notices, understands, tolerates, expresses, modulates, and recovers from emotions.

### Possible reported indicators

- Emotions feel overwhelming, confusing, numb, rapidly shifting, hard to express, or hard to recover from.
- Reliance on avoidance, suppression, withdrawal, escalation, self-criticism, or impulsive behavior when emotions rise.
- Difficulty identifying emotions or linking emotions to needs.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Emotion intensity or regulation strategy strongly predicts behavior and functioning.
- Client reports limited skills for naming, tolerating, or modulating emotions.
- Problems improve when emotion is identified, validated, paced, or regulated.
- Patterns occur across multiple settings or relationships.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Emotions are proportionate, understandable, and effectively expressed.
- Main barriers are practical, environmental, medical, or skill-specific rather than emotion regulation.
- Client recovers reliably and uses flexible strategies.

### Common maintaining processes

- Avoidance or suppression increases rebound intensity.
- Escalation produces short-term expression but long-term relational costs.
- Numbing reduces distress temporarily but limits learning and connection.
- Lack of emotion labeling reduces effective problem-solving.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “Which emotions are easiest and hardest to notice?”
- “What do you do when the emotion gets stronger?”
- “What helps the emotion move through, even slightly?”
- “What does the emotion seem to be asking for?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Emotion identification and validation.
- Distress tolerance and modulation skills.
- Opposite action or behavioral experiments when appropriate.
- Interpersonal expression and repair skills.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Sudden emotional lability, severe sleep loss, substance effects, medication changes, endocrine/metabolic concerns, or neurological changes may warrant assessment or consultation as clinically indicated.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Intense emotion does not establish a diagnosis.
- Emotion regulation work should not imply that distress is excessive when the client faces real threat, loss, injustice, or unmet needs.

## 8. Behavioral reinforcement and avoidance

### Plain-language definition

Behavior patterns that may provide short-term relief, reward, safety, or control while potentially maintaining distress, limiting learning, or increasing longer-term costs.

### Possible reported indicators

- Avoidance, withdrawal, procrastination, reassurance seeking, checking, overworking, conflict escalation, substance use, compulsive routines, reduced activity, or giving up valued actions.
- Short-term relief followed by longer-term regret, increased fear, lost opportunities, or relationship strain.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Clear short-term payoff and longer-term cost pattern.
- Behavior reliably follows specific emotions, thoughts, bodily cues, or relational triggers.
- Avoided activities become harder over time.
- Alternative behaviors reduce long-term cost when tried.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Behavior is adaptive, values-consistent, and matched to realistic constraints.
- Avoidance reflects actual danger or lack of access rather than a modifiable maintenance loop.
- No meaningful longer-term cost is identified.

### Common maintaining processes

- Negative reinforcement through relief after avoidance.
- Positive reinforcement through attention, control, stimulation, or short-term achievement.
- Reduced exposure to corrective information.
- Skill erosion or shrinking activity range over time.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What do you do next, and what does that do for you in the short term?”
- “What does it cost later?”
- “What are you avoiding that might matter to you?”
- “When have you responded differently, and what happened?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Behavioral activation.
- Exposure or approach planning when safe, indicated, and clinically appropriate.
- Reinforcement mapping and replacement behaviors.
- Motivational interviewing for ambivalence.
- Values-based action planning.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Avoidance or inactivity may be influenced by pain, fatigue, mobility limits, medication effects, substance use, sleep disruption, or medical restrictions; clarify before behavior-change planning.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Avoidance is not automatically irrational or pathological.
- Do not recommend approach strategies when danger, medical instability, or insufficient stabilization has not been addressed.

## 9. Identity and self-continuity

### Plain-language definition

The client’s sense of who they are, how they relate to past and future selves, and whether roles, values, agency, and life narratives feel coherent or disrupted.

### Possible reported indicators

- “I do not feel like myself,” “I do not know who I am now,” role loss, shame, identity conflict, life-transition distress, future disconnection, or difficulty integrating past experiences.
- Major transitions involving health, work, family, culture, migration, loss, trauma, caregiving, or relationship changes.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Distress centers on self-concept, role disruption, shame, agency, or future identity.
- Client describes a before/after rupture or difficulty connecting life chapters.
- Functioning improves when values, roles, and self-narrative become clearer.
- Identity themes appear in multiple concerns.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Sense of self remains stable and coherent despite distress.
- Main concern is circumscribed behavior, environment, physiology, or relationship conflict without identity disruption.
- Client has clear values and future orientation.

### Common maintaining processes

- Shame narratives reducing connection and action.
- Role loss narrowing perceived options.
- Avoidance of reminders of former or hoped-for self.
- Disconnection from values reducing motivation.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “How has this affected your sense of who you are?”
- “What feels continuous, and what feels disrupted?”
- “Which roles or values still feel important?”
- “What future self feels possible or difficult to imagine?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Values clarification and identity reconstruction.
- Self-compassion and shame resilience.
- Narrative integration and role transition support.
- Reconnection with meaningful activities and communities.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Sudden personality change, cognitive change, severe sleep loss, substance effects, medication changes, neurological concerns, or endocrine/metabolic concerns may warrant assessment or consultation as clinically indicated.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Identity disruption does not imply a specific disorder.
- Identity concerns may reflect realistic adaptation to loss, oppression, illness, disability, cultural transition, or role change.

## 10. Attachment and relational functioning

### Plain-language definition

Patterns in closeness, distance, trust, support, rupture/repair, boundaries, caregiving, dependency, conflict, and interpersonal safety.

### Possible reported indicators

- Fear of rejection, difficulty trusting, conflict cycles, withdrawal, pursuit/distance patterns, people-pleasing, boundary difficulty, isolation, caregiving strain, or limited support.
- Strong reactions to perceived disconnection, criticism, silence, or dependency needs.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Distress is repeatedly activated in relational contexts.
- Similar patterns appear across important relationships.
- Relationship responses maintain symptoms or functional difficulties.
- Support, repair, or boundary changes improve functioning.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Relational functioning is stable and supportive.
- Concerns occur independent of relational cues.
- Current relational distress is primarily due to realistic external conflict or unsafe behavior by others.

### Common maintaining processes

- Pursue-withdraw cycles.
- Fear-based testing, reassurance seeking, or emotional cutoff.
- Boundary collapse or rigid distancing.
- Conflict escalation reducing safety and repair.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What happens between you and others when this shows up?”
- “What do you most need in those moments, and what do you tend to do?”
- “Where do repair and support happen?”
- “Are there relationships where the pattern is different?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Interpersonal effectiveness and boundary skills.
- Attachment-informed pattern recognition.
- Communication, repair, and support-seeking.
- Family/systemic interventions when appropriate.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Relational changes that are sudden, uncharacteristic, associated with confusion, severe sleep loss, substance effects, medication changes, or neurological symptoms may warrant assessment or consultation as clinically indicated.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Relational patterns do not establish attachment style, personality diagnosis, or trauma history.
- Protective distancing may be adaptive in unsafe relationships.

## 11. Executive functioning

### Plain-language definition

Skills involved in planning, initiating, organizing, focusing, remembering, inhibiting impulses, shifting tasks, making decisions, and managing time, especially under stress.

### Possible reported indicators

- Procrastination, disorganization, missed appointments, difficulty starting tasks, losing track, impulsive choices, poor follow-through, decision paralysis, or overwhelm with multi-step tasks.
- Functioning worsens during stress, poor sleep, conflict, or high emotion.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Repeated executive-function difficulties across settings or task types.
- Difficulties interfere with goals, relationships, work, school, care tasks, or treatment follow-through.
- External structure, reminders, body doubling, reduced load, or stepwise plans improve functioning.
- Problems worsen with arousal, depletion, or environmental complexity.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Planning and follow-through are generally intact.
- Barriers are mainly lack of resources, unsafe environment, unclear goals, medical limits, or values conflict.
- Difficulties are temporary and resolve with situational changes.

### Common maintaining processes

- Avoidance after overwhelm leads to backlog and shame.
- Poor planning increases stress and reduces sleep/recovery.
- Disorganization produces interpersonal and occupational consequences.
- Self-criticism further reduces initiation.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “Where does the task break down: starting, sequencing, remembering, focusing, or finishing?”
- “What systems or supports help?”
- “How does stress or sleep affect this?”
- “Which tasks matter most right now?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Task breakdown and implementation intentions.
- Environmental supports, reminders, routines, and planning systems.
- Emotion regulation before problem-solving.
- Case-management support for external barriers.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Sudden cognitive change, confusion, head injury, severe sleep loss, medication/substance changes, neurological symptoms, or endocrine/metabolic concerns may warrant assessment or consultation as clinically indicated.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Executive difficulty does not establish ADHD, cognitive disorder, depression, or any diagnosis.
- Executive functioning may be constrained by poverty, discrimination, caregiving burden, unsafe housing, or excessive workload.

## 12. Environmental and social conditions

### Plain-language definition

External conditions that may shape distress, safety, access, resources, choices, and recovery, including housing, finances, work, school, legal issues, discrimination, community context, transportation, food security, and access to care.

### Possible reported indicators

- Housing instability, food insecurity, financial strain, workplace stress, school pressure, legal concerns, immigration stress, discrimination, lack of transportation, limited healthcare access, neighborhood safety concerns, or social isolation.
- Problems worsen when external demands or barriers intensify.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Distress and functioning track closely with environmental stressors.
- Practical barriers limit use of coping strategies or treatment access.
- Resource changes improve symptoms or functioning.
- Client identifies systemic or contextual constraints as central.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Stable, safe, and adequately resourced environment.
- Concerns persist similarly across contexts despite support and access.
- Environmental stressors are present but not central to the client’s goals or distress.

### Common maintaining processes

- Resource scarcity reducing choices and increasing stress.
- Unsafe or invalidating environments increasing threat detection and arousal.
- Access barriers preventing care, recovery, or social support.
- Individual-focused interventions missing structural drivers.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What outside pressures are shaping this?”
- “What resources are available, missing, or hard to access?”
- “Where are conditions unsafe, unstable, or invalidating?”
- “What practical change would reduce the load most?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Case management and resource linkage.
- Advocacy, care coordination, or documentation support within scope.
- Problem-solving around access barriers.
- Strengthening community and social supports.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Limited access to medical care, medications, nutrition, sleep, transportation, or safe housing may affect health and treatment planning; consider referral or coordination as clinically indicated.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Do not individualize distress that is meaningfully shaped by unsafe, unjust, or resource-limited conditions.
- Avoid implying that therapy skills alone can resolve structural barriers.

## 13. Strengths and protective factors

### Plain-language definition

Internal, relational, cultural, spiritual, practical, community, and historical resources that may support coping, recovery, meaning, safety, and change.

### Possible reported indicators

- Supportive relationships, values, faith/spirituality, cultural identity, creativity, humor, persistence, insight, prior coping successes, routines, exercise, meaningful work, caregiving motivation, community connection, or willingness to seek help.
- Exceptions when the concern is less intense or better managed.

### Information that would strengthen the hypothesis

- Strengths are accessible during stress or can be made more accessible.
- Protective factors have helped in similar situations.
- Client identifies values, relationships, or practices that increase safety, agency, or recovery.
- Supports can be mobilized without increasing risk.

### Information that would weaken the hypothesis

- Identified strengths are currently inaccessible, unsafe, depleted, or associated with additional burden.
- Supports are unreliable, coercive, invalidating, or conflictual.
- The client does not experience proposed strengths as meaningful.

### Common maintaining processes

- Strengths become disconnected from daily routines under stress.
- Shame or depletion reduces access to support.
- Overused strengths, such as responsibility or perseverance, become burdensome without boundaries.
- Protective relationships are underutilized due to fear, access barriers, or past disappointment.

### Appropriate clarification questions

- “What has helped even a little before?”
- “Who or what helps you feel more like yourself?”
- “Which strengths are available now, and which feel blocked?”
- “Are any strengths being overused in a way that costs you?”

### Possible therapeutic targets

- Strengths-based formulation and goal planning.
- Reconnection with values, routines, relationships, and community supports.
- Boundary-setting around overextended strengths.
- Using exceptions to design next steps.

### Relevant medical considerations

- Protective routines involving movement, sleep, nutrition, medication adherence, or health practices should remain within clinician scope and coordinate with medical care when appropriate.

### Cautions against overinterpretation

- Do not use strengths language to minimize risk, suffering, structural barriers, or referral needs.
- A listed support is not necessarily safe or protective without clarification.

## Cross-domain mapping and review requirements

## Rule structure

Each operational mapping rule should include:

- Rule ID and version.
- Intake field or entered fact pattern.
- Project Layers domain or domains activated.
- Tentative activation label.
- Rationale text.
- Supporting evidence display text.
- Missing-information prompts.
- Medical/referral consideration text when applicable, using phrases such as “Consider further assessment,” “Clarify medical history,” and “Coordinate with a medical provider as clinically indicated.”
- Contraindication or treatment-stage implications when applicable.

## Maintaining-cycle representation

A maintaining cycle should be represented as a provisional loop linking some or all of:

1. Context or trigger.
2. Activated Project Layers domains.
3. Appraisal, meaning, threat detection, bodily response, or relational cue.
4. Emotion or arousal state.
5. Behavior, avoidance, or interpersonal response.
6. Short-term consequence.
7. Longer-term maintenance cost.
8. Protective interruption point.

## Human review requirements

Clinical reviewers should approve:

- Domain definitions and indicators.
- Strengthening and weakening evidence fields.
- Medical/referral language.
- Clarification questions.
- Intervention targets.
- Cautions and contraindication implications.
- Export wording and disclaimers.
